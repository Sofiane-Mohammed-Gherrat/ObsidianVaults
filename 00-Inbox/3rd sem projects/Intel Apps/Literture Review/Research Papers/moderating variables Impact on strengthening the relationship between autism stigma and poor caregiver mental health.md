Based on the reference materials, the two categories of moderating variables are factors that influence the strength of the relationship between autism stigma and poor caregiver mental health [2][4]. By influencing the caregiver's mental state, these variables ultimately impact their capacity to provide high-quality care to the child with ASD.

***

### **Definitions of Moderating Variables**

#### 1. Changeable through Intervention
These variables are internal psychological states or skills that can be positively modified or enhanced through therapeutic interventions and support structures [2]. They represent areas where direct action (like therapy) can improve mental resilience.

*   **Examples:** Hopelessness, self-esteem, and self-compassion [2].

#### 2. Not Changeable
These variables are structural or contextual aspects of a caregiver’s life that exist regardless of intervention efforts. They create unique vulnerabilities for the individual caring for the child [2].

*   **Examples:** Gender, culture, financial burden, and time since diagnosis [2].

***

### **Development: Relationship to Quality of Care**

#### 🧠 Changeable Variables (Internal Focus)
These variables directly address the psychological resilience of the caregiver. When these are strengthened through intervention—such as by providing practical skills like self-compassion or mindfulness techniques—the caregiver’s mental health is protected from stigma [1].

*   **Impact on Care:** A mentally strong, resilient caregiver who has learned coping mechanisms is better equipped to handle daily stressors and social challenges. The research suggests that interventions producing a long-term positive effect by constructing "long-lasting support structures" are particularly powerful for maintaining the quality of care [1]. Furthermore, poor mental health in caregivers can lead to delayed developmental progress in the individual they care for, highlighting why intervening on self-esteem is vital for care quality [2].

#### 🧱 Not Changeable Variables (External/Structural Focus)
These variables define the external pressures and inherent circumstances that place a caregiver at risk. They do not disappear with therapy but require interventions to account for them.

*   **Impact on Care:** These factors determine *how vulnerable* the caregiver is. For instance, an informal caregiver facing both stigma **and** financial burden may be particularly susceptible to social isolation, which intensifies negative affect and undermines mental health [4]. Additionally, cultural knowledge surrounding ASD varies cross-culturally, meaning that the misconceptions held by a community (a non-changeable context) must guide how interventions are developed to be effective [3]. These variables require interventions that are tailored to local culture and socio-cultural settings [3].
