# Part 4: Literature Review: Caregiver Information Barriers and Systemic Obstacles

## 4.1 Introduction

When a child is diagnosed with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), caregivers rarely find themselves on stable footing; instead, they are usually dropped into a fragmented ecosystem where finding reliable information and immediate help feels incredibly uphill (Chu et al., 2020; Dobrogowska-Schlebusch, 2016; Mohd Hussain et al., 2022). This reality hits home particularly hard in Malaysia. Here, parents frequently struggle to make sense of the initial diagnosis, let alone figure out how to sequence therapy or keep the rest of family life from unraveling under the pressure (Chu et al., 2020).

We can map these overlapping struggles across five distinct areas. First, we look at the basic hurdles of baseline knowledge specifically, how a parent's educational background or income bracket subtly dictates the kind of information they can actually get their hands on (Chu et al., 2020; Dobrogowska-Schlebusch, 2016; Mohd Hussain et al., 2022). Second, there is the tricky issue of trust. Most families default to the internet for answers, but they are essentially sorting through an unvetted mix of genuine science, well-meaning blog posts, and outright myths (Dobrogowska-Schlebusch, 2016; Twombly et al., 2011; Mohd Hussain et al., 2022). Third, the review covers the emotional tax of caregiving, exploring how community stigma directly chips away at a family's social life and mental well-being (Papadopoulos et al., 2019; Chu et al., 2020). Fourth, we spot a noticeable blind spot in the current research: we don't really know how a caregiver's immediate emotional state <u>like panic or exhaustion or their language proficiency </u> (rule of 3) alters how they search for help. Finally, we look at structural brick walls, like traveling hours just to see a specialist or watching household finances crater from therapy costs (Kalkbrenner et al., 2011; Ou et al., 2015). Looking at these pieces together, it becomes clear that throwing more raw data at parents isn't the fix; we need tools that make existing support actually accessible.

## 4.2 Thematic Analysis

### Theme 1: Information and Knowledge Barriers

A closer look at the work of Chu et al. (2020), Dobrogowska-Schlebusch (2016), and Mohd Hussain et al. (2022) suggests that a caregiver's biggest informational hurdles usually pop up before they even type a query into a search bar. It is rarely a blank slate. Most parents start with very little baseline understanding of autism, and they are forced to navigate an environment where bad advice travels fast. But this isn't a uniform problem. Factors like socioeconomic status and formal education create an uneven playing field, meaning some families can filter through the noise much better than others. The real barrier, it seems, isn't that information doesn't exist. It is that systemic inequalities quietly restrict a parent's capacity to find, digest, and make sense of what is out there.

### Theme 2: Trust and Online Health Information

Finding a medical resource is one thing, but knowing whether to wager your child's developmental timeline on it is a completely different challenge. This becomes messy as more families turn to digital spaces. On one hand, the internet is a massive lifeline; nearly every modern ASD caregiver uses it, finding both clinical advice and a sense of shared community on forums or social media (Twombly et al., 2011). On the other hand, bypassing traditional medical channels leaves parents highly vulnerable to predatory health claims and pseudo-scientific cures (Dobrogowska-Schlebusch, 2016). They are left holding a tangled mix of evidence-based medicine and unverified internet rumors. Since the real-world value of a digital resource depends entirely on its accuracy (Mohd Hussain et al., 2022), simply making apps or portals more available misses the mark. Any practical digital framework needs to help families filter out the noise directly within the platforms they are already using.

**Theme 2: Trust and Online Health Information**

Accessing information is only part of the challenge; determining whether that information is trustworthy can be equally difficult. This issue becomes increasingly important as caregivers rely more heavily on digital platforms for ASD-related guidance. Twombly et al. (2011) found that online information-seeking is nearly universal among caregivers, with many families using digital spaces not only to obtain information but also to seek advice, reassurance, and social support from others facing similar experiences. Yet the widespread availability of online content does not necessarily translate into reliable guidance. As Dobrogowska-Schlebusch (2016) notes, barriers to accessing scientific information may leave caregivers more vulnerable to misinformation and unsupported health claims, particularly when evidence-based resources and unverified content are presented side by side. In practice, distinguishing between the two can be challenging, especially for caregivers who are already navigating unfamiliar medical terminology and intervention options. Mohd Hussain et al. (2022) further suggest that the value of digital resources depends not only on access but also on the quality and credibility of the information available. Taken together, these studies indicate that effective caregiver support may require more than simply increasing access to online information; it may also involve helping families identify, evaluate, and engage with trustworthy sources within the digital environments they already use.

### Theme 3: Stigma and Emotional Barriers

The challenges caregivers face cannot be understood in isolation from the psychological and social contexts in which they arise. Many studies consistently highlight increasing levels of stress and anxiety among caregivers of children with ASD, with social stigma only amplifying the problem. 

Notably, autism-related stigma has been shown to carry a severe negative impact on the mental health of informal caregivers who were defined here as any individual who provides substantial, day-to-day care and support for an autistic person within their personal or family setting outside of any formal or paid professional role (Papadopoulos et al., 2019).

Papadopoulos et al. (2019) further identified two categories of moderating variables that influence the relationship between autism stigma and caregiver mental health outcomes: those that can be addressed through intervention and training like: self-esteem, and those that remain unchanged, including gender and financial burden. This distinction is very important, as it suggests that while some risk factors are structural and difficult to change, others represent genuine entry points for support and these would be future features of AutiGuide.
