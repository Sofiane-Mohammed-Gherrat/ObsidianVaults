
For a standard Literature Review, your goal is not to classify *everything*; it is to **build an argument** that demonstrates what we know, what is debated, and—most importantly—what needs to be done next (your unique contribution).

I have reorganized the video’s concepts into three major phases: **Preparation**, **Mining**, and **Writing**.

---

## 🚀 Phase I: Preparation & Scoping (Before You Write)
*(Based on Stuckler's sections 01:21–02:40 and initial searching)*

This phase is about defining the conversation. Never start writing until you have completed these steps.

### 🎯 Step 1: Define the Conversation (The "Why")
*   **Concept:** Prof. Stuckler compares the LR to catching up with an old friend. You are bringing *yourself* and your reader up to speed on a field of knowledge.
*   **Action:** Write a clear, single-paragraph statement that answers: **"What is the big fuss about in this topic?"**
*   **Goal:** To establish the scope (what you will cover) and immediately identify the core problem or "knowledge gap." This Gap Statement is the *engine* of your entire paper.

### 📚 Step 2: Gather Material Strategically
*   **Concept:** Use search engines (like Google Scholar, as suggested) to find top papers in your field.
*   **Action:** Focus on finding foundational papers and recent "review" articles themselves (because they summarize others). Download a large pool of potential readings.
*   **Goal:** Build enough material to see patterns emerging *before* you start writing.

### 🔍 Step 3: Develop Your Conceptual Framework (The Skeleton)
*   **Concept:** Don't let your paper wander aimlessly. You must impose structure on the scattered knowledge. This framework will be the backbone of your review.
*   **Action:** After reading a few key papers, ask: "What is the natural way to group these findings?" Will you group them by **Time (Chronological)?** By **Topic/Theme?** Or by **Argument Type** (e.g., those who support causation vs. those who find only correlation)?
*   **Goal:** This framework determines your section headings (the structure of the body paragraphs).

## ⛏️ Phase II: Mining & Synthesis (The Data Gathering)
*(Based on Stuckler's sections 04:51–07:28)*

This phase is about deep reading and data extraction, not summarizing. You are acting like a detective lining up evidence.

### 📌 Step 4: Implement the "Stripping" Method
*   **Concept:** Do not summarize entire papers in your notes; extract only key pieces of information (key points, findings, arguments, quotes).
*   **Action:** Create a large, organized document or spreadsheet (your synthesis matrix). For every article you read, pull out:
    1.  The **main argument/hypothesis** of the authors.
    2.  Their **key finding**.
    3.  What they **suggested for future research**.
    4.  Any **contradictions or limitations** in their own study (This is gold for your discussion).
*   **Crucial Tip:** For every point you strip, keep the full citation next to it to prevent plagiarism and maintain academic integrity.

### 🧩 Step 5: Look for Patterns and Debate (Connecting the Dots)
*   **Concept:** The most valuable part of an LR is showing how papers relate to each other—they are not isolated units.
*   **Action:** After stripping, stop listing points by article. Start grouping them by **theme of debate**. If five articles make a point and three contradict it, your section structure must reflect this conflict. This shows intellectual maturity.

## ✍️ Phase III: Writing & Refining (The Drafting)
*(Based on Stuckler's sections 11:05–End)*

This phase follows a specific writing order to minimize writer’s block and maximize impact.

### 📖 Step 6: Write the Body Paragraphs First (Using PEER)
*   **Concept:** Use the PEER system to ensure every paragraph is focused, deep, and argumentative—not just descriptive.
*   **Action:** For every paragraph in your body sections, structure it rigidly:
    1.  **P (Point):** Start with a single, strong topic sentence that makes an argument ("Several studies confirm X..."). *Do not start with the citation.*
    2.  **E (Evidence/Examples):** Introduce 2-3 citations to support your point.
    3.  **E (Explanation/Analysis):** This is where you spend most of your energy. **Analyze** the evidence: Why did these authors reach this conclusion? How do their findings relate to others in the group? *This critical analysis is what makes a good LR.*
    4.  **R (Repeat/Link):** End by reinforcing the main point and smoothly transitioning to the next idea or section.

### 📜 Step 7: Write the Conclusion (Summary & Future Scope)
*   **Concept:** The conclusion must be predictive, not just descriptive. It closes the conversation loop you opened in the introduction.
*   **Action:** Structure your conclusion around these three parts:
    1.  **Recapitulation:** Summarize the main findings of your entire review (what is generally agreed upon).
    2.  **Limitations & Gaps:** State explicitly what was missing from the literature, *including* limitations in the evidence itself (e.g., "The existing studies only look at Western populations..."). This shows you are critical and highly knowledgeable.
    3.  **Future Research/Policy Implications:** Based on those gaps, tell your reader exactly what must be studied next or what policy change should occur.

### ✒️ Step 8: Write the Introduction (The Narrative Hook)
*   **Concept:** The introduction is the hardest part to write because it needs to bring the reader into a debate that hasn't happened yet—the debate you created through your synthesis.
*   **Action:** Write this *last*. Structure it with three parts:
    1.  **The Hook (Paragraph 1):** Bring the reader in by stating why the topic is important and controversial ("There are widespread concerns that X is happening...").
    2.  **The Problem/Gap (Paragraph 2):** State what specific knowledge gaps or debates exist (the "fuss about").
    3.  **The Thesis Statement (Paragraph 3):** Tell the reader precisely what your review will do to solve the gap ("Therefore, this review synthesizes X and Y literature in order to provide a conceptual framework for Z.").
